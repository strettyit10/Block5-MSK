Metabolic Bone Disease

LO 1

Characterize chemical and hormonal influences on bone

Key questions

  1. Q1What are the two jobs of bone?
    Structural (support, protection of viscera, locomotion and respiration, housing hematopoietic marrow) and metabolic (reservoir of calcium, phosphorus, sodium, magnesium and carbonate that keeps blood levels steady).
  2. Q2What is bone made of?
    Inorganic mineral — calcium hydroxyapatite — plus an organic part: bone cells and matrix proteins (mostly type I collagen, laid down as osteoid).
  3. Q3Who are the four cells, and what does each do?
    Osteoprogenitor cells (mesenchymal cells that become osteoblasts); osteoblasts (make matrix/osteoid); osteocytes (the most abundant bone cell; a canalicular network that senses mechanical force, regulates Ca²⁺/phosphate and signals formation vs resorption); osteoclasts (multinucleated, from monocyte–macrophage lineage, resorb bone).
  4. Q4How does woven bone become lamellar bone?
    Osteoblasts lay osteoid on a cartilage scaffold → woven bone; osteoclasts resorb it and osteoblasts replace it with stronger, organized lamellar bone. Woven bone in an adult always means a pathologic or reparative process.
  5. Q5What is the bone multicellular unit (BMU)?
    Osteoclasts + osteoblasts + blood supply + nerve supply + connective tissue working as a unit. Resorption and formation are tightly coupled, directed by osteocytes, and repair microdamage.
  6. Q6How does RANKL/OPG control resorption?
    RANKL on osteoblasts/stromal cells binds RANK on osteoclast precursors → osteoclast formation and activity. OPG (made by osteoblasts) is a decoy that binds RANKL and blocks it.
  7. Q7Which hormones push toward formation vs resorption?
    Estrogen and calcitonin inhibit osteoclasts. Vitamin D (1,25(OH)₂D) supports mineralization and osteoblasts. PTH acts on osteoblasts and raises RANKL → indirectly activates osteoclasts (net resorption when continuous).

Bone = hydroxyapatite + type I collagen + four cells. Osteoblasts make, osteoclasts resorb, osteocytes direct. RANKL → RANK drives osteoclasts; OPG is the decoy. Estrogen and calcitonin restrain osteoclasts; PTH raises RANKL; vitamin D mineralizes.

Memory hook

Blasts Build, Clasts Chew, Cytes Coordinate. Osteoclasts come from the same family as macrophages — both big eaters.

HY · Step 1
  • Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase = osteoblast activity marker (↑ in Paget, rickets/osteomalacia, healing fractures, osteosarcoma, blastic mets).
  • Osteoclasts are terminally differentiated — they do not divide; new ones come from monocyte precursors (they appear within ~1 week of a fracture).
  • Woven bone in an adult = pathologic (fracture callus, Paget, fibrous dysplasia, tumor).
LO 2

List factors affecting peak bone mass

Key questions

  1. Q8When is peak bone mass reached?
    In early adulthood — the 2nd to 3rd decade. After that, bone mass slowly declines.
  2. Q9What determines how high the peak is?
    Genetics, ethnicity, physical activity (mechanical loading), nutrition (calcium, vitamin D, protein) and hormonal status (estrogen/testosterone, GH).
  3. Q10How does timing of menarche affect peak bone mass?
    Early menarche = longer estrogen exposure → less resorption → higher peak bone mass. Late menarche, amenorrhea or early menopause lower it.
  4. Q11Why does peak bone mass matter?
    Osteoporosis later in life = how high you started minus how fast you lose. A low peak (poor diet, inactivity, early hypogonadism) is one of the three routes to osteoporosis.
  5. Q12What lowers bone mass after the peak?
    Age (fewer, less active osteoblasts and fewer growth factors), estrogen loss at menopause, inactivity, low calcium/vitamin D and secondary causes such as glucocorticoids.
  6. Q13What is the most practical way to raise peak bone mass?
    Weight-bearing exercise and adequate calcium/vitamin D before about age 30.

Peak bone mass is reached in the 2nd–3rd decade and set by genetics, ethnicity, activity, nutrition and hormones. Low peak + fast loss = osteoporosis.

Genetics / ethnicity+Weight-bearing activity+Ca²⁺, vitamin D, protein+Sex hormones→Peak bone mass (20s–30s)→ minus age + menopause losses →Fracture risk

Raises peak / slows loss

  • Weight-bearing and resistance exercise
  • Calcium + vitamin D + protein
  • Normal puberty and estrogen (early menarche = more estrogen exposure = higher peak)
  • Genetic background

Lowers peak / speeds loss

  • Inactivity, immobilization
  • Anorexia / amenorrhea, early menopause
  • Glucocorticoids, smoking, alcohol
  • Malabsorption (celiac, bariatric surgery)
Memory hook

Bone is a retirement account: deposit until ~30, then only withdrawals. Exercise before 30 is the best investment.

HY · Step 1
  • Female athlete triad (low energy, amenorrhea, low BMD) → low peak bone mass and stress fractures.
LO 3A

For each disease — deficient MATRIX: scurvy and osteogenesis imperfecta

Key questions

  1. Q14What goes wrong in scurvy?
    Vitamin C deficiency → inadequate hydroxylation of proline/lysine in procollagen → collagen cannot form a stable triple helix or cross-link → weak, easily degraded collagen.
  2. Q15Who gets scurvy?
    Elderly people living alone with poor diets; alcoholics (vitamin C is used up and intake is poor); dialysis patients (water-soluble vitamins are dialyzed out — uncommon); gastric bypass and other malabsorption; fad dieters; infants on unsupplemented formula.
  3. Q16How does scurvy present?
    Bleeding from fragile vessels (perifollicular purpura, bleeding gums), poor wound healing, joint pain/swelling from hemarthrosis, subperiosteal hemorrhage; anemia. X-ray: ground-glass osteopenia with subperiosteal hemorrhage.
  4. Q17What skeletal changes occur in children with scurvy?
    Defective osteoid → cartilage overgrows into the marrow → bowing of long bones, depressed sternum, outward-projecting ribs.
  5. Q18What is the defect in osteogenesis imperfecta?
    Mutations in COL1A1/COL1A2 (α1 and α2 chains of type I collagen); rare recessive forms involve CRTAP or LEPRE1. "Too little bone" — brittle bones.
  6. Q19Which organs does OI affect and why?
    Everywhere type I collagen matters: bone (many fractures), joints (laxity), skin, eyes (blue sclerae — thin sclera shows the choroid), ears (conductive + sensorineural hearing loss), teeth (dentinogenesis imperfecta: gray-blue/yellow-brown, fragile teeth).
  7. Q20How do the OI types differ?
    Type I: AD, ↓ amount of normal collagen, postnatal fractures, blue sclerae, normal stature (most common, mildest). Type II: perinatal lethal. Type III: progressive deforming, short, sclerae blue → white. Type IV: moderate, short stature, normal sclerae.
  8. Q21What is a classic OI trap?
    Multiple fractures in a child can look like abuse — look for blue sclerae, dental problems, hearing loss and family history.

Matrix problems = bad collagen. Scurvy: no vitamin C → no hydroxylation → bleeding gums, purpura, subperiosteal hemorrhage. Osteogenesis imperfecta: type I collagen gene mutation → fractures + blue sclerae + hearing loss + bad teeth.

Memory hook

OI = "I can't BITE": Bones (fractures), I (eyes — blue sclerae), Teeth (dentinogenesis imperfecta), Ears (hearing loss).

HY · Step 1
  • Vitamin C is a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase (in the RER) — also needed for iron absorption and dopamine → norepinephrine.
  • OI can be mistaken for child abuse; the sclerae and teeth give it away.
  • Achondroplasia (FGFR3 gain of function) is not a collagen disease — short limbs, normal trunk, normal bone density.
  • A different FGFR3 activating mutation causes thanatophoric dysplasia: very short limbs and a small chest that restricts lung growth → lethal pulmonary hypoplasia around birth.
LO 3B

For each disease — deficient MINERALIZATION: hyperparathyroidism and rickets/osteomalacia

Key questions

  1. Q22What does PTH excess do to bone?
    Increases osteoclast activity → resorption, especially of cortical bone (subperiosteal resorption of the radial side of middle phalanges, distal clavicle; osteopenia at the distal forearm/wrist).
  2. Q23What are the two classic bone findings in hyperparathyroidism?
    Dissecting osteitis — osteoclasts tunnel into the center of trabeculae ("railroad track" appearance). Brown tumors — masses of multinucleated giant cells (osteoclast-like), hemorrhage (hemosiderin → brown) and reparative fibrous tissue. Advanced disease = osteitis fibrosa cystica.
  3. Q24How do primary, secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism differ?
    Primary: autonomous adenoma (most) or hyperplasia → ↑ Ca²⁺, ↑ PTH, ↓ phosphate. Secondary: low Ca²⁺ or vitamin D deficiency (often CKD) drives compensatory ↑ PTH with low/normal Ca²⁺. Tertiary: after long secondary disease the glands become autonomous → ↑ PTH and ↑ Ca²⁺.
  4. Q25How does primary hyperparathyroidism present?
    "Stones, bones, groans, moans": kidney stones and hypercalciuria, fractures/osteopenia, abdominal pain, and neuropsychiatric symptoms ("brain fog"). Often found by incidental hypercalcemia. Sestamibi scan localizes the adenoma.
  5. Q26What is rickets vs osteomalacia?
    The same defect — failure to mineralize osteoid — in children (rickets, open growth plates) vs adults (osteomalacia, closed growth plates).
  6. Q27What are the causes?
    Vitamin D problems (poor sun exposure/dark skin/low intake; malabsorption — celiac, gastric bypass, biliary or pancreatic disease; liver disease; renal failure or 1α-hydroxylase defect; P450 inducers such as phenytoin, rifampin, phenobarbital; vitamin D receptor defects) or phosphate depletion (antacids, X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets).
  7. Q28What does rickets look like?
    Growth plate cartilage keeps accumulating and thickens; chondrocytes lose columns; hypertrophic zone expands. Clinically: bowed legs, rachitic rosary (costochondral beading), craniotabes (soft skull), frontal/parietal bossing, pigeon chest, waddling gait. X-ray: cupping and fraying of metaphyses.
  8. Q29What does osteomalacia look like?
    Excess unmineralized osteoid (wide osteoid seams) → bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, fractures (pseudofractures/Looser zones).
  9. Q30What are the labs in vitamin D–deficiency rickets?
    Low 25-OH vitamin D, low/normal calcium, LOW phosphate, HIGH PTH (secondary hyperparathyroidism), HIGH alkaline phosphatase.

Mineralization problems. Hyperparathyroidism: osteoclasts eat cortical bone → dissecting osteitis ("railroad tracks") and brown tumors; primary = ↑ Ca²⁺ ↑ PTH. Rickets/osteomalacia: not enough vitamin D or phosphate → unmineralized osteoid; ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH, ↑ ALP.

Memory hook

Brown tumors are brown from hemosiderin — old hemorrhage among osteoclast-like giant cells. Railroad tracks = osteoclasts tunneling down the middle of a trabecula.

HY · Step 1
  • Primary HPT: ↑ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH, ↑ urinary cAMP; subperiosteal resorption of the radial side of the middle phalanges.
  • Renal osteodystrophy = secondary HPT from CKD (↓ 1α-hydroxylase, phosphate retention).
  • X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets: FGF23 excess → renal phosphate wasting; normal Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate, ↓ 1,25-D.
  • Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital) induce P450 → vitamin D breakdown → osteomalacia.
LO 3C

For each disease — impaired REMODELING: osteoporosis, osteopetrosis and Paget disease

Key questions

  1. Q31What is osteoporosis?
    Reduced bone mass with increased porosity and fracture risk. Cancellous (trabecular) bone is hit first: trabeculae thin and lose their connections → microarchitectural instability.
  2. Q32What are the three routes to osteoporosis?
    Failure to reach peak bone mass (genetics, nutrition, lifestyle), increased breakdown (estrogen deficiency, hyperparathyroidism, low Ca²⁺/vitamin D, inactivity) and inadequate formation (aging — impaired cell renewal, fewer growth factors).
  3. Q33Primary vs secondary osteoporosis?
    Primary = postmenopausal (estrogen loss → ↑ RANKL, ↓ OPG, ↑ IL-6) and senile. Secondary = multiple myeloma; endocrine (hyperthyroid, hyperparathyroid, hypogonadism, Cushing, growth hormone deficiency); GI (celiac, bariatric surgery, liver disease); renal osteodystrophy; drugs (corticosteroids, aromatase inhibitors, chemo, anticoagulants, anticonvulsants); immobility.
  4. Q34What are its complications?
    Vertebral compression (anterior wedge) fractures → back pain, height loss, kyphosis, 5× risk of more spine fractures. Hip fracture → 8–34% higher 1-year mortality, 2.5× future fracture risk, 20% need long-term nursing care. Distal radius (Colles) fractures.
  5. Q35What is osteopetrosis?
    Defective osteoclast resorption — often a defect in acidifying the resorption pit (carbonic anhydrase II) or in osteoclast activation (e.g., RANKL pathway). AR forms are severe (infancy); AD form is mild and found later ("marble bone").
  6. Q36What does osteopetrosis look like?
    Diffusely sclerotic, dense bones on X-ray; histology shows persistent primary spongiosa (calcified cartilage cores), woven bone without lamellar bone and no marrow space. Bones are dense but brittle → fractures.
  7. Q37What are the consequences of no marrow space?
    Pancytopenia/anemia, infections, extramedullary hematopoiesis → hepatosplenomegaly; narrowed foramina → cranial nerve palsies (vision, hearing, facial).
  8. Q38What is Paget disease (osteitis deformans)?
    Localized, disordered remodeling in three phases: osteolytic → mixed osteoclastic/osteoblastic → osteosclerotic ("burnt out"). Net bone mass rises, but the bone is thick, weak and prone to fracture and deformity.
  9. Q39How does Paget present?
    Older adult (usually >40–50, often Northern European descent), frequently asymptomatic with isolated ↑ alkaline phosphatase (normal Ca²⁺ and phosphate) and increased uptake on bone scan. Skull, spine, pelvis and lower-limb long bones. Bone pain, warmth over bone, enlarging hat size, hearing loss, spinal stenosis, bowing, fractures, high-output heart failure (AV shunting).
  10. Q40What are the classic Paget findings?
    X-ray: "cotton-wool" skull, mixed lytic/sclerotic lesions, thickened cortex. Histology: mosaic pattern of lamellar bone with irregular cement lines; huge osteoclasts with many nuclei and intranuclear inclusions; marrow replaced by vascular fibrous tissue.
  11. Q41What causes Paget, and what is its worst complication?
    Possibly paramyxovirus infection (measles, RSV) → IL-6 → osteoclast activation, plus genetics (SQSTM1 and other loci). Complications include secondary osteosarcoma (and giant cell tumor).
  12. Q42Why are Paget osteoclasts overactive?
    Their precursors are hypersensitive to vitamin D and RANKL, and IL-6 released by bone cells recruits even more osteoclasts.

Remodeling problems. Osteoporosis: too little bone, normal labs, trabecular loss → vertebral wedge and hip fractures. Osteopetrosis: osteoclasts can't resorb → dense, brittle bone, no marrow → anemia, infections, cranial nerve palsies. Paget: chaotic remodeling → thick, weak bone, isolated ↑ ALP, cotton-wool skull, mosaic lamellar bone, risk of osteosarcoma.

Memory hook

Paget = "PAGEs of a book stacked badly" — a mosaic of cement lines. Isolated ↑ ALP in an older adult = Paget until proven otherwise. Osteopetrosis = bone like petrified stone — hard but shatters.

HY · Step 1
  • Osteoporosis labs are normal; diagnosis by DXA (T-score ≤ −2.5) or fragility fracture.
  • Osteopetrosis: carbonic anhydrase II mutation → osteoclasts can't acidify; treat severe infantile form with bone marrow transplant (osteoclasts come from marrow monocytes).
  • Paget: hearing loss (ossicles/CN VIII), ↑ hat size, high-output heart failure, osteosarcoma; treat with bisphosphonates.
  • Mosaic bone pattern on histology is a favorite lab image for Paget.
  • Paget is a disease of older adults (usually >50) — not children.

Application questions

1. An 81-year-old man who lives alone and eats only frozen microwave dinners has bleeding gums, purpura over the shins, painful swollen knees and a normocytic anemia. X-ray shows ground-glass osteopenia with subperiosteal hemorrhage of the femur. Which step of collagen synthesis is defective?

  1. Transcription of COL1A1
  2. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues
  3. Glycosylation of hydroxylysine
  4. Cleavage of procollagen peptides
  5. Cross-linking by lysyl oxidase (copper)
Answer
B. Scurvy: vitamin C is the cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase; without hydroxylation the helix is unstable and vessels bleed.

2. A 14-year-old girl has mixed conductive and sensorineural hearing loss, yellow-brown bell-shaped teeth and steel-gray sclerae. At age 30 she fractures her femur after a fall; DXA shows osteopenia everywhere. Most likely mechanism?

  1. Deficient HGPRT activity
  2. Failure of type I collagen formation by osteoblasts
  3. FGFR3 activation in the growth plate
  4. Increased IL-6 from osteoblasts
  5. Reduced vitamin D receptors
Answer
B. Osteogenesis imperfecta (type I): defective type I collagen → brittle bones, blue-gray sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta and hearing loss.

3. A 58-year-old woman has hypercalcemia on routine labs, elevated intact PTH, "brain fog," increased urinary calcium and a sestamibi scan with uptake in the right inferior parathyroid. A bone biopsy would most likely show:

  1. Mosaic lamellar bone with cement lines
  2. Osteoclasts tunneling through the centers of trabeculae
  3. Persistent calcified cartilage with no marrow
  4. Wide unmineralized osteoid seams only
  5. Empty lacunae in a subchondral wedge
Answer
B. Primary hyperparathyroidism: dissecting osteitis ("railroad track" trabeculae) ± brown tumors.

4. A 4-year-old picky eater who avoids dairy and sun has bowed legs, a waddling gait, pectus carinatum and proximal weakness. X-rays show cupping and fraying of the distal radius. Which lab pattern fits?

  1. ↑ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH, normal ALP
  2. ↓/N Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate, ↑ PTH, ↑ ALP
  3. Normal Ca²⁺, phosphate, PTH and ALP
  4. ↑ Ca²⁺, ↑ phosphate, ↓ PTH, ↓ ALP
  5. Isolated ↑ ALP only
Answer
B. Vitamin D–deficiency rickets: low phosphate, secondary hyperparathyroidism and high alkaline phosphatase.

5. A 42-year-old man has hip and knee pain. X-rays show generalized sclerosis and increased radiodensity. Labs later show anemia and splenomegaly. What is the underlying defect?

  1. Excess osteoblast activity driven by IL-6
  2. Inability of osteoclasts to acidify and dissolve hydroxyapatite
  3. Deficient type I collagen
  4. Unmineralized osteoid from vitamin D deficiency
  5. Excess RANKL expression by tumor cells
Answer
B. Osteopetrosis: osteoclasts cannot resorb (e.g., carbonic anhydrase II defect) → dense, brittle bone with no marrow space.

6. A 78-year-old woman using hearing aids has an elevated total and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium and phosphate. Her left tibia is bowed and warm; skull X-ray shows cotton-wool spots. Which histologic finding is expected?

  1. Mosaic pattern of lamellar bone with prominent cement lines
  2. Sheets of small round blue cells
  3. Thin, disconnected trabeculae only
  4. Needle-shaped crystals in neutrophils
  5. Caseating granulomas
Answer
A. Paget disease of bone: disordered remodeling produces a mosaic of lamellar bone; giant osteoclasts; marrow replaced by vascular fibrous tissue.

7. A 65-year-old woman with celiac disease hears a "pop" while opening a window and has acute low back pain. Lateral X-ray shows an anterior wedge deformity of L2. Serum calcium, phosphate and PTH are normal. Which bone compartment is primarily affected by her disease?

  1. Cortical bone of the diaphysis
  2. Cancellous (trabecular) bone
  3. Articular cartilage
  4. Periosteum
  5. Growth plate
Answer
B. Osteoporosis (here with a secondary cause — malabsorption) primarily thins and disconnects trabecular bone → vertebral compression fractures.
