Soft Tissue Pathology

LO 1

Characterize soft tissue tumors in terms of incidence, etiology and risk factors

Key questions

  1. Q1What counts as "soft tissue"?
    Non-epithelial tissue outside bone, brain and organs: fibrous tissue, fat, smooth and skeletal muscle, peripheral nerve and its sheaths, and blood/lymphatic vessels.
  2. Q2How common are soft tissue tumors, and which are most common?
    The great majority are benign (lipoma is the most common soft tissue tumor). Sarcomas (malignant mesenchymal tumors) are rare — that rarity makes them hard to recognize and easy to mistreat.
  3. Q3Why are they hard to diagnose?
    Hundreds of entities, most made of look-alike spindle cells (elongated cells, longer than wide — the hallmark cell; scan at low power to see the growth pattern, then look for secondary features such as inflammation, hyalinization or necrosis); classification (WHO) now combines morphology, immunohistochemistry and molecular testing.
  4. Q4What causes most soft tissue tumors?
    Unknown for most. Some benign lesions follow trauma or foreign material (nodular fasciitis may follow trauma).
  5. Q5What are the risk factors for sarcoma?
    Prior radiation (latency ~5–10 years), chemicals (vinyl chloride and thorium dioxide → angiosarcoma of the liver; herbicides; alkylating chemotherapy), viruses (HHV-8 → Kaposi; EBV → smooth muscle tumors in immunosuppressed), chronic lymphedema (angiosarcoma) and familial syndromes (NF1 → MPNST; Gardner/FAP → desmoid; Li-Fraumeni; hereditary retinoblastoma).
  6. Q6What do sarcoma karyotypes look like?
    Simple karyotype (~20%): one recurrent translocation, monomorphic cells, younger patients (e.g., synovial sarcoma, myxoid liposarcoma, alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing). Complex karyotype (~80%): many gains/losses, genomic instability, pleomorphic tumors in older patients.
  7. Q7Where do sarcomas arise?
    Mostly deep tissues: extremities (especially the thigh), retroperitoneum and abdomen, trunk, genitourinary tract, head and neck.

Soft tissue = fat, fibrous tissue, muscle, nerve, vessels. Most tumors are benign (lipoma most common); sarcomas are rare and usually deep (thigh, retroperitoneum). Risk: radiation, chemicals (vinyl chloride), viruses, lymphedema, NF1, Gardner, Li-Fraumeni. ~20% simple translocation karyotypes (young), ~80% complex (older, pleomorphic).

LO 2

Discuss the approach to the patient with a soft tissue mass: presentation, diagnostic procedures and ancillary tests

Key questions

  1. Q8What history matters?
    Age, duration, rate of growth (sudden enlargement), pain or neurologic symptoms, history of trauma, prior radiation, lymphedema and family syndromes.
  2. Q9Which features raise concern for sarcoma?
    Size >5 cm, deep location (below the fascia), rapid or recent growth, firm/fixed mass, older patient, retroperitoneal site. (A fatty mass in the retroperitoneum is a liposarcoma, not a lipoma, until proven otherwise.)
  3. Q10What does imaging do?
    CT/MRI localizes and characterizes (fat, myxoid, mineralization, necrosis, vascularity), guides biopsy and plans definitive surgery; many deep tumors are only found on imaging.
  4. Q11Which biopsy is preferred?
    Percutaneous core needle biopsy (or open incisional biopsy when more tissue is needed). FNA yields individual cells rather than a tissue core, so it is less informative for a first diagnosis. Excisional biopsy is reserved for very small lesions or a known lipoma.
  5. Q12Why avoid "shelling out" a possible sarcoma?
    Excisional biopsy or enucleation contaminates tissue planes, leaves tumor behind, and makes later limb-sparing wide excision harder — "unnecessary complications."
  6. Q13What ancillary tests help?
    Immunohistochemistry for lineage (desmin/myogenin for muscle, S-100/SOX10 for nerve sheath, CD31/ERG for vessels, keratin/EMA and TLE1 for synovial sarcoma, CD99 for Ewing, MDM2 for liposarcoma) and cytogenetics/molecular tests for signature translocations.
  7. Q14Who should manage these patients?
    An experienced multidisciplinary team — surgeon, radiologist, pathologist, oncologist — because the tumors are rare and errors are costly.

Big (>5 cm), deep, growing, or retroperitoneal = worry about sarcoma. Image first (MRI/CT), then core needle or incisional biopsy — never shell out a possible sarcoma. Confirm lineage with IHC and signature translocations.

LO 3

Discuss basic principles of surgical resection and grading of soft tissue tumors

Key questions

  1. Q15What is the most important prognostic factor for a sarcoma?
    Histologic grade (incorporated into TNM staging) — along with size, depth and margins.
  2. Q16How are sarcomas graded?
    By necrosis, cellularity, pleomorphism and mitotic count (atypical mitoses point to high grade). Low grade = bland, few mitoses; high grade = pleomorphic, mitotically active, necrotic.
  3. Q17Which sarcomas have an inherent grade?
    Many: well-differentiated liposarcoma is grade 1 by definition; rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma and (by convention) synovial sarcoma are high grade.
  4. Q18What is the surgical principle?
    Wide excision with a cuff of normal tissue around the tumor (limb-sparing when possible) — never "shell out" along the pseudocapsule. Radiation adds local control; chemotherapy is standard for metastatic (stage IV) disease, adjuvant use is controversial.
  5. Q19How do sarcomas spread?
    Hematogenously — most often to the lungs, then bone. Lymph node spread is unusual.
  6. Q20Which sarcomas break the rule and go to lymph nodes?
    Rhabdomyosarcoma, clear cell sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma (and angiosarcoma).

Grade (necrosis, cellularity, pleomorphism, mitoses) is the key prognostic factor; some sarcomas are graded by definition. Treat with wide excision (cuff of normal tissue, limb-sparing) ± radiation. Sarcomas spread hematogenously to lung; lymph node exceptions: rhabdomyosarcoma, clear cell, synovial, epithelioid.

LO 4A

Lesions: FIBROUS (nodular fasciitis, fibromatosis) and LIPOMATOUS (lipoma and variants, liposarcoma), with Gardner syndrome

Key questions

  1. Q21What is nodular fasciitis?
    A benign, self-limited reactive (pseudosarcomatous) proliferation of fibroblasts/myofibroblasts, often after trauma. Rapidly growing, small (1–3 cm), sometimes tender nodule in the subcutis — most often the forearm — of young adults (20–40).
  2. Q22How is nodular fasciitis recognized under the microscope?
    Plump, uniform (myo)fibroblasts in a loose "tissue culture–like," feathery pattern with microcystic spaces, extravasated red cells and scattered lymphocytes; mitoses may be frequent but are not atypical. It carries t(17;22) MYH9-USP6. Often mistaken for sarcoma and sent for second opinion; does not recur after excision.
  3. Q23What are fibromatoses?
    Benign but infiltrative fibroblastic proliferations of adults with a tendency to recur and never metastasize: firm, poorly circumscribed masses of bland fibroblasts in long sweeping fascicles with abundant collagen that infiltrate surrounding muscle.
  4. Q24What are the superficial fibromatoses?
    Small, slow, arising from fascia/aponeuroses: palmar (Dupuytren — flexion contracture of the 4th/5th fingers), plantar (Ledderhose — non–weight-bearing sole) and penile (Peyronie — plaque on dorsal/lateral shaft → curvature).
  5. Q25What are the deep fibromatoses (desmoid tumors)?
    Large, faster-growing, infiltrative: extra-abdominal (shoulder, chest wall, pelvis, thigh of adolescents/young adults), abdominal wall (pregnant or postpartum women, often within a year of childbirth) and intra-abdominal (mesentery/pelvis — associated with FAP/Gardner). Locally aggressive, recur, do not metastasize; mutations in β-catenin (CTNNB1) or APC.
  6. Q26What is Gardner syndrome?
    An autosomal dominant FAP variant (APC mutation): colorectal adenomatous polyps with near-certain colon cancer, plus osteomas (skull/jaw), soft tissue tumors (fibromas, lipomas, desmoids), epidermal cysts, dental abnormalities (unerupted/supernumerary teeth) and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (bilateral pigmented fundus lesions). Loss of APC → β-catenin accumulates → uncontrolled proliferation.
  7. Q27What is a lipoma?
    The most common soft tissue tumor: benign, encapsulated, soft, mobile, slowly growing subcutaneous mass of mature fat (back, shoulder, neck, proximal extremities), usually adults 40–60. Simple excision cures it; it does not recur.
  8. Q28Which lipoma variants should you know?
    Angiolipoma (fat + small capillaries with fibrin thrombi; young adults, forearm; often multiple and painful), chondroid lipoma (cartilage-like matrix; rare, women) and spindle cell/pleomorphic lipoma (spindle cells and "floret" giant cells; posterior neck/shoulder of older men).
  9. Q29What is liposarcoma and how is it diagnosed?
    The most common adult sarcoma: a malignant fatty tumor of deep tissues (thigh, retroperitoneum), never arising from a lipoma. The key cell is the lipoblast — a primitive fat cell with bubbly, vacuolated cytoplasm scalloping a compressed nucleus.
  10. Q30What are the liposarcoma types?
    Well-differentiated (looks like fat with fibrous bands and atypical stromal cells; MDM2 amplification; recurs, especially retroperitoneal) ↔ dedifferentiated (high grade, aggressive). Myxoid ↔ round cell (t(12;16) FUS-DDIT3; 30–40s, thigh; gelatinous myxoid matrix, lipoblasts, delicate "chicken-wire" capillaries; the more round cells, the worse the prognosis; tends to metastasize to other soft tissue sites). Pleomorphic (rare, high grade).
  11. Q31How does round cell liposarcoma differ grossly from myxoid?
    Round cell is solid and opaque on cut section; myxoid is gelatinous.

Nodular fasciitis: young adult, forearm, grows fast, tissue-culture fibroblasts with extravasated RBCs, benign, no recurrence. Fibromatosis: bland fibroblasts + collagen that infiltrate and recur — Dupuytren (palm), Ledderhose, Peyronie; desmoid = deep (postpartum abdominal wall; Gardner/FAP mesentery). Lipoma: most common, superficial, cured by excision. Liposarcoma: deep thigh/retroperitoneum, lipoblasts; myxoid type = t(12;16), chicken-wire capillaries.

Memory hook

Retroperitoneal fat = liposarcoma, not lipoma. Nodular fasciitis is "fast but friendly" — grows in weeks, never comes back. Desmoid is "slow but stubborn" — infiltrates and recurs.

HY · Step 1
  • Gardner syndrome = FAP (APC) + osteomas + desmoids + epidermoid cysts + dental anomalies + CHRPE.
  • Dupuytren contracture: palmar fascia → flexion of ring/little fingers; associations include alcohol use, diabetes, Northern European men.
  • Lipoblast = diagnostic cell of liposarcoma (vacuoles indent the nucleus).
LO 4B

Lesions: SKELETAL MUSCLE (rhabdomyosarcoma) and VASCULAR (hemangioma, angiosarcoma)

Key questions

  1. Q32What is rhabdomyosarcoma?
    The most common soft tissue sarcoma of childhood: a malignant tumor showing skeletal muscle differentiation, often in sites with little skeletal muscle (head and neck, genitourinary tract). Desmin and myogenin/MyoD1 positive; rhabdomyoblasts may show cross-striations. High grade by definition.
  2. Q33What is embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma?
    The most common subtype (~50%), children under 10: head and neck (orbit, nasopharynx) and genitourinary tract; resembles stages of developing skeletal muscle, from small round blue cells to strap cells.
  3. Q34What is the botryoid variant?
    An embryonal subtype arising beneath the mucosa of hollow organs (vagina of young girls, urinary bladder): grape-like polypoid mass; defined by a cambium layer — a dense subepithelial band of tumor cells separated from intact surface epithelium by a loose zone.
  4. Q35What is alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma?
    ~20%; adolescents and young adults (10–25); deep soft tissue of the extremities and head and neck; discohesive tumor cells clustered around fibrous septa, mimicking lung alveoli; t(2;13) PAX3-FOXO1 or t(1;13) PAX7-FOXO1.
  5. Q36What is pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma?
    Rare adult form (men >60), deep thigh; large, bizarre eosinophilic cells. Spindle cell type occurs at any age and site.
  6. Q37What is a hemangioma?
    A benign tumor of endothelial cells, most common in infancy/childhood, usually head and neck and superficial (can occur in the liver). Classified by vessel caliber.
  7. Q38Capillary vs cavernous hemangioma?
    Capillary (including infantile "strawberry" hemangioma): red, raised, appears soon after birth, grows then usually regresses; small, plump endothelial cells with inconspicuous lumens. Cavernous: less common, larger, deeper, poorly circumscribed, bluish; large dilated blood-filled channels with flat endothelium; does not regress; large lesions can trap platelets (consumptive thrombocytopenia).
  8. Q39What is angiosarcoma?
    An aggressive malignant tumor of blood or lymphatic endothelium: atypical endothelial cells forming irregular, anastomosing vascular channels; as grade rises the lumens become poorly formed and atypia increases. CD31/ERG positive.
  9. Q40What are the clinical settings of angiosarcoma?
    1) Primary cutaneous — elderly, scalp/face, bruise-like indurated patches. 2) Lymphedema-associated — chronic lymphedema after mastectomy/axillary dissection (Stewart-Treves): red-brown nodules that ulcerate and bleed, or skin discoloration. 3) Post-radiation — breast, usually within ~5 years. 4) Deep soft tissue — any age; 1/3 with predisposing conditions. 5) Parenchymal organs — liver (vinyl chloride, arsenic, Thorotrast), bone.

Rhabdomyosarcoma: child's most common sarcoma; head and neck + GU; desmin/myogenin. Embryonal (most common; botryoid = grape-like, cambium layer, vagina/bladder) vs alveolar (teens, extremities, cells around septa, t(2;13)). Hemangioma: infant, capillary regresses, cavernous doesn't. Angiosarcoma: elderly scalp, chronic lymphedema, post-radiation; ulcerating red-brown nodules.

HY · Step 1
  • Rhabdomyosarcoma: desmin, myogenin, MyoD1; most common sarcoma in children; botryoid in the vagina of girls <5.
  • Stewart-Treves: angiosarcoma in chronic post-mastectomy lymphedema.
  • Hepatic angiosarcoma: vinyl chloride, arsenic, Thorotrast. Kaposi sarcoma: HHV-8.
  • Cavernous hemangioma of the liver is the most common benign liver tumor; don't biopsy (bleeds).
LO 4C

Lesions: PERIPHERAL NERVE SHEATH (schwannoma, neurofibroma) with NF1 and NF2, and UNKNOWN HISTOGENESIS (synovial sarcoma)

Key questions

  1. Q41What is a schwannoma?
    A benign, encapsulated nerve sheath tumor made entirely of Schwann cells, growing eccentrically on the side of the nerve (the nerve can be spared at surgery). S-100 positive.
  2. Q42What does a schwannoma look like?
    Alternating cellular Antoni A areas (spindle cells with nuclear palisading — Verocay bodies) and loose, myxoid Antoni B areas; hyalinized vessels. Usually solitary and sporadic; vestibular (acoustic) schwannomas at the cerebellopontine angle; bilateral = NF2.
  3. Q43What is a neurofibroma?
    A benign, unencapsulated tumor of mixed cells — Schwann cells, fibroblasts and perineurial cells — that grows through the nerve and incorporates axons, in a loose myxoid/collagenous ("shredded carrot") stroma.
  4. Q44What forms do neurofibromas take?
    Localized cutaneous (soft, solitary, common, often sporadic), localized intraneural (fusiform nerve enlargement with residual axons), diffuse, and plexiform (many fascicles of a large nerve expanded — "bag of worms"). Plexiform neurofibromas are essentially diagnostic of NF1 and can become malignant (MPNST); diffuse neurofibromas are sometimes NF1-associated.
  5. Q45What is NF1?
    Autosomal dominant (1 in ~2,500–3,000), 17q11.2, neurofibromin — a GTPase-activating protein that turns off RAS; loss → RAS stuck "on." Variable expressivity, complete penetrance.
  6. Q46What are the features of NF1?
    Café-au-lait macules, axillary/inguinal freckling (by age 3–5), neurofibromas (cutaneous and plexiform), Lisch nodules (iris melanocytic hamartomas, highly specific), optic pathway glioma, skeletal dysplasia (tibial bowing, sphenoid wing dysplasia), scoliosis; learning disabilities/ADHD in ~50%; ~10% lifetime risk of MPNST; also pheochromocytoma, GIST, glomus tumor, arterial stenoses.
  7. Q47What is NF2?
    Autosomal dominant, much rarer (1 in ~35,000), chromosome 22, merlin (schwannomin) — a cytoskeletal/Hippo-pathway protein needed for contact inhibition. Hallmark: bilateral vestibular (acoustic) schwannomas; also meningiomas, ependymomas, cutaneous schwannomas and juvenile posterior subcapsular cataracts.
  8. Q48What is synovial sarcoma?
    A sarcoma of uncertain histogenesis — despite the name it does not arise from synovium. Young adults (15–40), deep soft tissue near large joints (especially around the knee). Biphasic (epithelial glands + spindle cells) or monophasic spindle; calcifications common. Keratin/EMA and TLE1 positive; t(X;18) SS18-SSX in >90%. High grade; can spread to lymph nodes and lungs.

Schwannoma: encapsulated, pure Schwann cells, eccentric on the nerve, Antoni A (Verocay) / Antoni B; bilateral acoustic = NF2 (chr 22, merlin). Neurofibroma: mixed cells through the nerve; plexiform = NF1 (chr 17, neurofibromin → RAS), café-au-lait, freckling, Lisch nodules, MPNST risk. Synovial sarcoma: young adult near the knee, biphasic, t(X;18) SS18-SSX.

HY · Step 1
  • Schwannoma and neurofibroma are S-100+ (neural crest). Verocay bodies = schwannoma.
  • Lisch nodules are specific for NF1; café-au-lait spots alone are not.
  • Synovial sarcoma: t(X;18), biphasic, near joints but not from synovium; keratin+ (epithelial differentiation).

Application questions

1. A 20-year-old woman has a 6-cm firm, poorly demarcated mass in the rectus abdominis that must be excised with a muscle margin. It shows bland fibroblasts in broad sweeping fascicles amid dense collagen. She and a sibling had colectomies for polyposis. Diagnosis?

  1. Desmoid tumor (deep fibromatosis)
  2. Myositis ossificans
  3. Neurofibroma
  4. Nodular fasciitis
  5. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma
Answer
A. Gardner syndrome/FAP: intra-/abdominal desmoids — infiltrative, recur, never metastasize.

2. A 57-year-old woman cannot fully extend her left middle finger; a firm cord is palpable in the palm. What is most abundant in the lesion?

  1. Atypical spindle cells
  2. Dystrophic calcification
  3. Fibroblasts with collagen
  4. Granulation tissue
  5. Lipoblasts
Answer
C. Dupuytren contracture = superficial (palmar) fibromatosis: bland fibroblasts and dense collagen.

3. A 47-year-old man has a 10-cm solid mass deep to the quadriceps. Karyotype shows t(12;16)(q13;p11). Most likely diagnosis?

  1. Chondrosarcoma
  2. Liposarcoma
  3. Metastatic adenocarcinoma
  4. Nodular fasciitis
  5. Rhabdomyosarcoma
Answer
B. Myxoid/round cell liposarcoma: t(12;16) FUS-DDIT3, deep thigh; lipoblasts and chicken-wire capillaries.

4. A 33-year-old man has a 2.5-cm tender forearm lump that appeared within the past 2 weeks. Biopsy shows plump fibroblasts in a loose, tissue-culture–like pattern with extravasated red cells and some mitoses. Best next step?

  1. Wide excision with radiation
  2. Chemotherapy
  3. Reassurance: this is a benign reactive lesion
  4. Amputation
  5. Genetic testing for APC
Answer
C. Nodular fasciitis: fast-growing, benign, reactive; commonly mistaken for sarcoma; does not recur.

5. A 4-year-old girl has a grape-like mass protruding from the vagina. Biopsy shows a dense layer of primitive tumor cells just beneath intact epithelium. Which stain would be positive?

  1. S-100
  2. Myogenin/desmin
  3. CD31
  4. Cytokeratin
  5. CD99 only
Answer
B. Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, botryoid variant (cambium layer); skeletal muscle markers desmin and myogenin.

6. A 74-year-old woman had a mastectomy with axillary dissection 15 years ago and has had arm lymphedema since. Red-brown nodules now appear on the arm, some ulcerating and bleeding. Diagnosis?

  1. Kaposi sarcoma
  2. Angiosarcoma
  3. Cavernous hemangioma
  4. Recurrent breast carcinoma only
  5. Pyogenic granuloma
Answer
B. Lymphedema-associated angiosarcoma (Stewart-Treves).

7. A 25-year-old man has a slowly enlarging mass near the knee joint. Biopsy shows both gland-like epithelial structures and spindle cells; keratin is positive. Which genetic change is expected?

  1. t(X;18) SS18-SSX
  2. t(11;22) EWSR1-FLI1
  3. t(2;13) PAX3-FOXO1
  4. MDM2 amplification
  5. NF1 loss
Answer
A. Biphasic synovial sarcoma — young adult, near large joints, t(X;18).
